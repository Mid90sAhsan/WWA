If you drive to your customers, you probably don’t want your home address on Google Maps. You don’t have to show it. Google has a setting for exactly this, called a service-area business. Getting it right keeps your address private and keeps your profile out of trouble. Getting it wrong is one of the most common reasons I see profiles suspended.

## Storefront or service area: what the two settings mean

Every Google Business Profile is one of three types:

- **Storefront.** Customers come to you. Your address shows on Maps with a pin. Think of a salon, a dentist, a shop.
- **Service-area business.** You go to customers. Your address is hidden, and Google shows a shaded area instead. Think of a plumber, a cleaner, a mobile groomer.
- **Hybrid.** Both. Customers can visit, and you also travel to them. Your address shows, and you list service areas too. Think of a landscaper with a garden center.

## Google’s rule, in plain words

Show your address only if customers can come to that address and be served by your staff during the hours you list.

That’s the whole test. If people don’t walk in, you’re a service-area business, and the address should be hidden. It doesn’t matter whether you work from home, a garage, a truck or a rented yard. A sign on the building doesn’t change it either.

## The mistakes that get profiles in trouble

- **Showing a home address as a storefront.** If customers never visit, Google treats it as misleading. Plenty of owners do this to “look established”, and it’s a common trigger for suspension.
- **Using a virtual office, coworking desk or mailbox address.** A UPS Store box or a virtual office address doesn’t count as a business location, even if it looks like a real street address. Google catches these, often during video verification.
- **Making extra profiles for each city you serve.** One business, one profile. A second “office” you don’t actually staff counts as a fake listing.
- **Drawing the service area across the whole state.** Google’s guidance is to list the areas you really serve, roughly within two hours’ drive of where you’re based. Bigger doesn’t get you more calls. It just looks less believable.

If your profile is already suspended for one of these, [this note on suspensions](/notes/suspended/) walks through the appeal.

## How to switch to a service-area business

1. Sign in to the Google account that manages your profile and open it (search your business name on Google, or use Google Maps).
2. Go to **Edit profile**, then **Location**.
3. Turn off showing your business address to customers, or clear the address if Google asks you to.
4. Open **Service area** and add the cities, towns or zip codes you actually work in.
5. Save, then leave it alone while Google reviews the change.

Changing location settings can trigger a review, and sometimes a new round of verification. That’s normal. If it asks you to verify again, [here’s how verification works](/notes/verification/). Keep a work vehicle, tools and your license handy in case it asks for a video.

## How to pick your service areas

Google lets you list up to 20 areas. You don’t need to use them all.

- **Start with where your jobs actually come from.** Check your last few months of invoices. Those towns go first.
- **Use names people search for.** A city or a well-known town name works better than an obscure neighborhood.
- **Be honest about distance.** If you’d turn down a job in a town, don’t list it.

## What hiding your address doesn’t change

Hiding your address changes what customers see, not how Google ranks you. Google still knows where you’re based, and distance still counts. Your service area tells customers where you’ll travel. It won’t put you in the map results in a town 40 miles away. For why distance matters so much, read [the map pack, in plain English](/notes/near-me/).

So a plumber based in one suburb will usually show up best near that suburb, whatever the service area says. To win work further out, your website matters more than the map. A real page for each area you serve, with jobs you’ve done there, beats a service area drawn around half the state.

## On your website

Your site should agree with your profile.

- **If you’re a service-area business,** you don’t need a street address in the footer. Your business name, phone number and the areas you serve are enough.
- **List your areas in plain words,** for example “Serving Columbus, Dublin, Westerville and Hilliard.”
- **Don’t make 40 copies of the same page** with only the city name swapped. A few real area pages, each with something specific to that place, are better than many empty ones.

## What I would do this week

- Check whether customers ever visit your address. If not, hide it.
- Remove any virtual office or mailbox address from your profile.
- List the 5 to 10 towns most of your jobs come from, and set those as your service areas.
- Make sure your website shows the same business name, phone and areas.
- Make the change in one sitting, then stop editing while Google reviews it.

Getting this setting right protects your privacy and your profile at the same time.
