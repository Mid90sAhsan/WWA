You search your own name. Nothing. Or the pin is across town. Or a closed shop still owns the listing. This is common. It is also usually fixable — if you treat Maps as a listing you own, not a lottery.

Google does not “hide” you for fun. Something in the profile, the address, or the site is confusing it. Here is what I check first.

## 1. There is no Google Business Profile — or it isn’t yours

Maps listings come from a Google Business Profile. If nobody claimed it, Google may have built a stub from directories, or shown nothing. If someone else claimed it — an old owner, a vendor, a random “manager” — you are locked out until you prove the business is yours.

Fix: search the business on Google, open the profile, and use “Own this business?” If you already have an account, confirm you can edit hours and photos. If you can’t, start the reclaim flow and keep the postcard or video verification handy.

## 2. The profile is suspended

A suspended profile disappears from Maps. Common triggers: a name stuffed with keywords (“Best Plumber Cityname 24/7”), a fake address for a service-area business, sudden bulk edits, or a listing that looks like a duplicate.

Fix: put the real legal or storefront name back. Use the service-area setting if you go to customers and don’t have a public office. Then request a review from inside the profile. Don’t make a second listing to “start over” — duplicates make this worse.

## 3. The pin is in the wrong place

Google drops the marker from the address you typed. A suite number, a shared building, a rural route, or an old unit can put you on the wrong block. Customers who trust the map walk into someone else’s door.

Fix: in the profile, adjust the map marker to the door people actually use. Match that address on your site footer, invoices, and directories. One name, one address, one phone — written the same way everywhere.

## 4. Google thinks you’re a different kind of business

Categories decide which searches you can even enter. Primary category is the job. Extra categories are supporting. If the primary is wrong — “Consultant” instead of the trade or care you actually sell — you lose the searches that pay.

Fix: pick the most specific primary category that is still true. Add only extras you really offer. Then write the description like a person: what you do, where, for whom. Not a keyword pile.

## 5. The site and the listing don’t agree

Your site says one city. The profile says another. The Facebook page has an old number. Google notices the disagreement and gets cautious. Cautious listings rank worse, or not at all.

Fix: pick the canonical name, address, and phone. Update the site first. Then the profile. Then the few directories that already mention you. Don’t buy fifty new citations overnight. Consistency beats volume.

## 6. There’s nothing for Google to trust

Empty profiles look unfinished. Hours missing, no photos, no posts, no reviews, a website that doesn’t load on a phone. Distance still matters — a shop across the street can beat you — but a thin profile loses to a complete one nearby.

Fix, in order: correct hours (including holidays). Real photos of the place, the work, and the people. A site that opens fast and repeats the same address. Then ask customers who already like you for a review. Never buy reviews. Google is good at spotting that, and owners talk.

## What I would do this week

- Confirm you can edit the profile. If not, reclaim it.
- Set the real name, the right pin, and the true primary category.
- Match name, address, and phone on the website.
- Add hours and a handful of honest photos.
- If the site is slow or broken on a phone, fix that before you chase rankings.
